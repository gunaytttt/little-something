Little Something

Money, wrapped with care.

Little Something lets you give money as a gift you design yourself, with a photo, a background, a note and a reveal moment, so it still feels like a gift after it's opened. Money is often the right gift. But sent digitally, it arrives as a bank transfer with nowhere to put the effort, so it feels impersonal.

How it works

A mobile-first web app for giving money as a gift. The giver makes the gift first and adds money second, so the effort comes before the amount.

For the giver
01

Make the gift

A title, a note, a photo, a background and an effect. The note can say what the money is for, or nothing at all. The app nudges ("Go on, get mushy") but never writes it.

02

Add money

Then the amount and payment. Money screens stay quiet, with plain type and exact numbers.

03

Preview and send

A last look at the gift exactly as the receiver will see it, then the giver decides how it goes out: a link through any chat or email, a printed card with a QR code that folds like a greeting card or slips into an envelope, or scheduled for later. More money can still be added here.

For the receiver
04

Open it

No app and no account. The link opens on a sealed reveal, then the look, the note and the amount.

05

Move it to your bank

The money waits in the gift until they're ready. How they spend it is up to them, and the giver only ever sees "Opened".

Also in the product

A home screen where givers see their drafts and the status of each gift (Draft, Ready to send, Sent, Opened), and an optional Receiving tab where receivers keep their gifts.

Left out on purpose

Group collection, brand-locked spending and AI-written notes.

TL;DR

A frustrating group gift made me ask why there's no good way to give money that still feels like a present. I took that question from first research to a designed product, with a business model and a plan to measure it.

Part 2

How I got here

Why I started

It started with a friend's birthday. A group of us were chipping in for an expensive check-up she wanted, and I was the one collecting the money. A bank transfer was the most practical option.

But sending it straight to her bank account felt inconsiderate, even though it was what she wanted.

It would merge with everything else in her account, and I know from experience how easily gift money gets spent on other things. Once that happens, the reason behind the gift is lost.

How I handled it
  1. SearchedOnly gift cards and prepaid cards. Nothing let me give digital money as a present.
  2. Made a cardDesigned it in PicsArt and printed it for the party.
  3. Sent the moneyA separate transfer that evening. It only worked because I had her bank details.
Why this problem

I kept wondering why nothing like this existed. When I decided to do a self-directed product case study for my move into product management, I didn't want to make up a problem. I already had one I had lived through, so I started there: what exists, and why is this gap still open?

The problem and who it's for

Many people want money as a gift and appreciate the freedom it gives, and 49% would like to receive digital cash but feel weird asking for it (Wells Fargo/Ipsos, 2025). The problem is how it's presented and perceived in a social context.

In person, we put money in an envelope or a card and say why we're giving it: "Go treat yourself" or "This is for the guitar you wanted." Digitally, the same gift becomes a bank transfer or a payment link, and there's nowhere to put the effort. That's when a money gift starts to feel impersonal. In the same survey, 57% said a digital gift can feel like no effort went into it.

Today people work around it. Brand-locked gift cards are more intentional but take away the freedom, and prepaid cards feel thoughtless on their own. Both are easily lost, left at home or forgotten. Some people improvise, folding cash into origami or printing their own vouchers.

The survey is a US sample of 2,010 adults: evidence for the category, not proof it works in Europe.

Givers

Defined by the moment, not by age: they've already decided money is the right gift and want it to carry effort. For example, when they:

  • are close to someone but too far away to hand over a gift. This is where I'd start.
  • want to put money toward something specific, like a camera.
  • know someone well enough to give a gift, but not well enough to pick one.
Receivers

Friends and family, whatever bank they use. They never need the app: the gift arrives as a link and lives on their phone, so it can't get lost. The money is theirs to spend on anything, whenever they're ready, and the note and photo stay as a keepsake.

Not for

People who love choosing gifts and know exactly what someone wants, or givers who want to control how the money is spent. Little Something is another option for the moments when a present doesn't fit.

What I learned

Four interviews, about ten Reddit threads where people debate money as a gift, the Wells Fargo/Ipsos survey and a competitor map. Four findings shaped the product most.

The demand is bigger than I expected

Found
On Reddit, people openly ask how to give money without it feeling impersonal.
Changed
Confidence the problem was worth solving, and that awkwardness, not the money itself, is what to design for.

Nobody owns the giving moment

Found
Collecting and moving money are already solved, but nobody owns the giving moment. Could an app still stand out there? Partiful did it in a solved market by making the experience fun. And in Asia, money gifts like Hongbao and wedding envelopes are already a ritual, and Hongbao has moved into apps.
Changed
I positioned on the experience: the giver designs the gift before adding money.

AI writing the note would break the gift

Found
One interviewee said AI help with the message would remove the very effort the gift is meant to show.
Changed
The app prompts but never writes for the giver. AI can help behind the scenes, never as the voice of the gift.

Gift cards get forgotten

Found
Every interviewee had forgotten or lost a gift card, or never found the time to use it.
Changed
The gift lives on the receiver's phone, with an optional reminder.

What my research can't tell me yet

I spoke to a few people I know, most of my sources are qualitative, and the one quantitative survey is from the US. I don't have European numbers or a market size. I also can't tell what amount people are comfortable giving. €15 in money can feel small, while a €15 book feels thoughtful because nobody checks the price. People might also say they'd use the product and then hesitate when it comes to sending €25.

Next I would test real amounts with the working product, look for European data on gifting money, and ask receivers and givers how they feel about privacy.

Key decisions and trade-offs

1

Compete on the experience, not on moving money

The question

After the market scan, I asked myself whether this was worth building at all. The market looked crowded. But every product I found sat in one of two places: flexible but impersonal, or personal but tied to brands. Nobody offered both.

Why

Moving and collecting money are solved. What isn't is everything around it: to give money with a few words today, you send a transfer and then work out where the words go, in a chat, a call or a line in the transfer description.

What I chose

To compete only on the experience of giving: the intent and the reveal. Moving the money stays with whoever owns the product, a bank in my lead path.

What I gave up

Group collection. My own story started with a group gift, but Tikkie, bank payment requests and Collection Pot already do it well, so building it would mean competing where I have no edge.

What would change my mind

Testing that shows a money gift still reads as low effort even with a look and a note, or that it only works where money gifting is already a ritual.

2

No account to start, open or receive

The question

Where should sign-up sit? Most money apps ask for an account first. If I'm competing on experience, giving has to feel at least as easy as a bank transfer, and receiving easier than a gift card.

What I chose

Givers start as a guest and log in only when they add money. Receivers never need an account: they open the link and confirm a quick code to move the money. An account is offered later, as an option, to keep their gifts in one place.

Why

For givers, it's adoption. People drop off when asked to sign up for something they don't understand yet, but once they've made the gift, they're likely to sign up to send it. For receivers, it's the moment. Handing over your details turns a gift into an obligation. And a receiver who enjoyed it is the most likely next giver.

What it costs

Fewer accounts and less data on receivers. Money still needs a check when it moves, so no account never means no verification.

What would change my mind

Guest drafts that rarely get finished, a bank that needs more than a code to pay a receiver, or an A/B test showing an account up front doesn't hurt sign-ups, gifts sent or receivers becoming givers.

3

The giver sees "Opened", and nothing after that

The tension

Givers hope a gift gets used the way they meant it, but the point of money is freedom. If someone gave you money for a guitar and a friend then passed you their old one, you should be able to spend it on something else without explaining yourself.

What I chose

"Opened" and nothing after it, the digital version of seeing someone unwrap your present. Everything after that belongs to the receiver.

What it costs

The giver can't see when a gift sits unused, and forgotten gift cards came up in every interview. For now the receiver sets their own reminder, and the giver can always just ask.

Where it fits

The need is real, but money gifting on its own makes very little money. What's expensive is the setup around it: on my own, a licensed payment partner's minimums mean about 8,000 gifts a month just to cover fixed costs (my estimate from published pricing), before building trust from zero. What's different is the experience. So the question is who it's worth the most to: a company that already moves money and wants a reason for people to choose it.

My lead

A standalone app owned by a bank that wants to be closer to people's everyday lives. Think of a bank that's strong in mortgages and business banking but rarely the app people open for everyday money. For that bank, Little Something is mostly a brand play: a warm moment that shows it's there for people, not only for big decisions. The app keeps its own brand and works with any Dutch bank, while the bank holds the licence and moves the money, the way Tikkie works for ABN AMRO.

First audience

People in the Netherlands sending gifts to friends and family elsewhere in Europe. It's the strongest giver moment I found, and euro to euro needs no currency exchange and no new rules.

What the bank gets

The giver pays, never the receiver, and the core experience stays free. Money comes from around it: brand offers tied to what the gift is for, and later a small giver fee. The bigger gain is reach beyond the bank's own customers and a reason for receivers to open an account, measured as new accounts per gift against what the bank usually pays to win a customer.

The biggest risk

Separate bank apps don't always survive. Zelle shut down its standalone app in 2025 once banks offered the same thing inside their own apps. The ones that last do a job the main banking app can't. For Little Something, that job is the experience: the look, the note and the reveal.

Ruled out

Selling to businesses, since cash to employees counts as salary in the Netherlands, and earning from gifts nobody opens, which clashes with EU e-money rules.

Success metrics

The team would steer by one number, gifts opened per month, and the bank would judge the product by what each new customer costs.

Every target here is a rough estimate, anchored where I could on research into similar situations rather than identical apps. The sources and the math are in my metrics working notes.

Bank outcome

What each new account costs

(yearly running cost + cost per gift × gifts) ÷ new accounts

At low volume, running costs (team, support, marketing) are much bigger than the cost per gift, so each account gets cheaper as gifts grow. The product earns its place once this falls below what the bank usually pays to win a customer, a figure I'd get from the bank.

North star

Gifts opened per month

Opening is the moment both sides get something: the giver's effort lands and the receiver gets the gift. For a receiver who isn't a customer, it's also the first time they meet the bank. Gifts sent only counts the giver's half. New accounts is what the bank wants, but if the team chased it, the product would start asking for sign ups during the reveal, which goes against my decision on accounts.

Inputs

Each input is tied to a decision, so if one moves the wrong way, I know which decision to revisit. Gifts happen a few times a year, not every week, so the time windows are long.

Guardrails

What must not get worse

  • Payment success rate stays at 98% or higher (assumption). A failed payment hurts more in a gift than in a transfer, because it breaks a moment.
  • Drop off at "move it to your bank" doesn't rise. It's the one step where the receiver is asked for anything.
  • The effort score doesn't fall, measured with a skippable one question survey after sending and after moving the money.
  • Support contacts per 1,000 gifts don't rise.
Stop signs

What would make me rethink

  • Fewer than 10% of givers send another gift within 12 months. People try it once and don't come back, so the experience isn't worth repeating and competing on it doesn't hold.
  • Fewer than 5% of receivers become givers in the first year. Growth would then depend on paid marketing, which weakens the case for the bank.
  • The cost per new account stays above the bank's usual cost. Then the feature inside a bank app or the transfer app partner becomes the better owner.

How I'd test it

Research panels before launch, then a product built to run experiments from day one.

Before launch: research at scale

Before there's real traffic, I'd test the prototype with panels of people living in the Netherlands, recruited through a research platform such as Maze or Lyssna. After launch: built to experiment

Day one

Experiment-ready

Every key feature ships behind a feature flag in a tool like GrowthBook or PostHog, so it can be tested or rolled back at once. Connected to Claude Code, an AI agent drafts each experiment and I approve it before it goes live.

The bank's gain

A holdout

A share of receivers never sees the bank account offer. The difference between the groups shows how many accounts the gifts create, rather than accounts people would have opened anyway.

Reading results

Bold tests, read honestly

Early traffic can only detect big effects, so I'd test real changes and read them with sequential testing instead of stopping at the first good day. Small choices, like the order of occasion templates, go to a bandit that shifts traffic to what works on its own.

Regulated

Compliance first

Anything that touches fees, verification or payment limits gets a compliance review before it's tested.

The phases overlapped more than a timeline suggests. Research, assumptions and interviews ran side by side, each one sharpening the others. The PRD kept changing after design started: the home screen and draft saving only became must haves once I saw the screens. And I designed in grayscale first, adding the brand only once the flow and every feature were designed.

What I'd do differently

I left the business model until the product felt clear. Looking back, deciding the launch path earlier would have changed things. Knowing it would be bank owned, I wouldn't have gone as deep into payment vendors, I would have checked the rules for building inside a bank sooner, and I might have put digital wallets like Apple Pay and Google Pay, and cards, first in the design.

The lesson I'm taking with me: the business model is an input to design, not something to add at the end.
